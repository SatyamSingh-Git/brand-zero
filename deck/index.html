02The bottleneck

Think9's twelfth brand launches next month. Nobody knows what will work.

It sits in ingestible wellness — the category The Good Bug, SuperYou and Panchamrit are already in. The team must pick the message for its launch ads and put budget behind it. They believe it will lift sales about 35%.

What the lift could really be
−24% to +140%
A campaign that loses money, or one that nearly doubles sales. Both are consistent with what they know today.
Cost of getting this one call wrong
₹5.01L
On ₹40L riding on the decision. Not a forecast of revenue — the expected cost of calling it the wrong way.
And this repeats
× 19
Eleven brands live, thirty planned. Nineteen more launches, each paying close to full price for the same lesson.

The bottleneck is not "knowledge is fragmented." It is launch decision latency — the weeks and rupees between "we're launching X" and "we know what works" — paid again for every brand. Today that learning travels through people in meetings: slow, lossy, and it stops working around brand seven.

03Why the obvious answer fails

The obvious fix is worse than building nothing.

Pool all eleven brands, take the average, serve one number. This is the shape most "central AI brain" proposals quietly take. It does not make the answer more accurate — the estimate is still just the portfolio average. It only makes the answer sound certain. And certainty is the part that makes people spend.

What it claims
±11 pts
A tight, confident, specific planning range
How far off it typically is
±164 pts
About 14× wider than it admits
How often it is actually right
7%
It tells you 80%. Truth lands inside about one launch in fourteen
Brand Zero, same question
−5% to +91%
Wider, and honest about it
Why this matters more than any win we could show. A wide honest answer sends the team to go and test. A narrow confident wrong one sends them to spend. Comics and probiotics do not share a price response — and a system that averages them together will never tell you so. Knowing when not to pool is not a feature of this system; it is the reason it exists.
Measured, not asserted: hold-one-brand-out coverage for naive pooling lands at 0–5% across both decision classes in backtest.py.

05Organ 1 — the Ledger

A claim, not a document. And not all claims count the same.

Two decisions carry most of the weight, and both are the kind of thing a system gets wrong silently.

  • Everything lands on one scale. A price elasticity in face wash and one in a ₹4,000 device are not the same quantity. Effects arrive in whatever units the source used — a 1.19 conversion ratio, a 0.07 fractional change — and are converted to one common axis before anything is pooled. Skip this and the model does confident arithmetic on incompatible numbers.
  • Weak evidence still counts — it just counts less. A randomised holdout and a line in a pricing meeting are both evidence. Each tier discounts how much a claim can move the answer. Nothing is thrown away; nothing weak can move a posterior on its own.
  • Every number traces back. A claim without a link to its source is rejected by the schema. Any answer the system gives can be followed to the Slack message or spreadsheet row it came from, in two clicks.
Where it came fromCounts as
Randomised holdout / clean A-B100%
Geo split, time split70%

Correlational read15%
Said in a meeting5%
The same claim, two ways. A pricing-meeting opinion with a nominal error of 0.40 enters the model at 1.79. It is kept, it is auditable, and it cannot move the answer by itself.
schema.py — 208 lines. Most Slack messages are opinions, not causal claims; the extractor's first job is to reject.

08Organ 3 — the Allocator

Fund the test the portfolio learns most from. It is rarely the biggest brand.

The constraint nobody designs around: you can only run an experiment on a brand that is already live. Pre-launch brands carry the most risk and have no way to test. So "which live brand should we test" has an answer nobody computes — test the live brand that is the best lens on the launches queued behind it, even when that brand is small.

PolicyPicksLearning boughtTo others
Biggest brand (what happens today)brand 01₹0.01L—
Each P&L optimises its ownbrand 03₹0.24L0%
Brand Zerobrand 00₹1.87L97%

Brand Zero's pick ranks 25th of 25 by revenue at stake — literally the last brand anyone would think to test. It wins because the brands it teaches cannot yet be tested at all.

The honest half. Across 12 draws it picks a different test than "fund the biggest brand" in 5. In the other 7, the flagship's own call happens to sit near its decision threshold and instinct is right. It earns its place on the draws where instinct is wrong, not by being different every time.
The governance that makes it shippable

Telling Brand 7's lead "your budget went to Brand 12 for portfolio value" gets this switched off inside a quarter. They carry a P&L.

So the Allocator owns no brand budget. It owns a small ring-fenced learning budget and bids — offering to fund tests inside brands. Brands opt in because it is free money.

Same mathematics. Survivable politics.

allocator.py — expected value of information by Gauss–Hermite quadrature. The cross-brand covariance it needs falls straight out of the fit; no second model.
09Organ 4 — Calibration

The one organ that pays at eleven brands. And at one.

Everything else needs neighbours before it works. This needs only that people write down what they expect before they find out — and get scored afterwards. The model forecasts too, on the same scale, scored the same way, with no special treatment.

ForecasterSays 80%Really isRange width
Brand Zero80%82%0.25
Lead A80%90%0.40
Lead B80%5%0.12

Lead B is not a worse operator. They state 12-point ranges on a question that moves 60 points. The score notices. That is a conversation about uncertainty, not about judgement — and it needs no sibling brands, which is why it ships in week one.

  • It is a proper scoring rule. Quoting enormous ranges to be safe costs you on width; quoting tight ones costs you on misses. Honest reporting is the way to win.
  • Within two quarters you know who is calibrated on what. Not who is smart — who is well-calibrated on price versus creative versus channel. Those are different people.
  • It turns judgement into evidence. A calibrated operator's opinion can enter the ledger with a defensible weight instead of a guessed one.
  • It is also the honesty mechanism. When the system refuses to answer and an operator overrides it — which is often correct — the override automatically opens a prediction in their name. Overriding is free. Overriding without saying what you expect is not.
calibration.py — Winkler interval score. Ninety seconds per launch decision, and it is the only part of this that works before the portfolio grows.
10Does it work

Hold one brand out. Predict it cold. Repeat for all of them.

Remove every observation belonging to one brand, fit on the rest, predict it from nothing — exactly the position a brand is in on launch day. The number to watch is coverage: a range sold as 80% should contain the truth about 80% of the time.

It has nothing to add there and does not pretend to. A system that won everywhere would be the less believable one.
What this does not show

These numbers are synthetic. The world they come from was generated with the same three-level structure the model fits. That demonstrates the mechanism; it is not evidence Think9's portfolio behaves this way.

The protocol that would settle it is this exact backtest pointed at the real ledger — a week-3 measurement, and it is in the plan. If it comes back weak, that is a finding to act on, not to explain away.

backtest.py — runs in CI, not by hand, because an evaluation that runs when someone remembers stops running in week five.
11Architecture

Where claims come from, and where humans decide.

Sources — all of them already exist
  • Meta / Google Ads — creative tests, the cleanest high-tier evidence
  • Shopify, Amazon, Flipkart — price, pack, cohort revenue
  • Slack, meeting transcripts, vendor WhatsApp — the reasoning no dashboard holds
  • Broadway & Brand Bridge — 350+ third-party brands and 10k+ stores Think9 observes but does not own. The answer to cold start: the prior for brand 12 need not come only from eleven brands.
Agents — one job each
  • Extractors — Claude with the claim schema as a forced output format, so a malformed claim is impossible. First job is to reject.
  • Adjudicator — dedupes, resolves conflicts, assigns the evidence tier
  • Prior service — nightly refit, per decision class
  • Query agent — answers in Slack, cites its claims, refuses when it should
  • Allocator — weekly ranked bid list. Never moves money.
Human checkpoints — six
  • Claim confirmation — brand lead, one click. Ignored → enters one tier lower.
  • Tier promotion — only a named human can call something randomised. Tiers fall automatically; they never rise.
  • Refusal override — allowed, logged, opens a prediction
  • Experiment funding — weekly council. Nothing funded by inaction.
  • Pre-registration — the one hard gate, above a spend threshold
  • Audit — any number, back to its source, in two clicks
Each checkpoint specifies what happens by default if nobody acts — because in practice most checkpoints resolve by default, and a design that ignores that is decoration. Stack: Postgres · Airbyte · Claude with structured outputs · numpy · Prefect · FastAPI + Slack. Deliberately no RAG and no vector store — the unit is a typed claim and the retrieval key is structured, so a semantic layer would be reaching for a familiar tool rather than the right one.

1230 days

Sequenced by what pays at eleven brands — so pooling ships third.

Week 1
The ledger exists, and pre-registration goes live
One Postgres table. 20–30 past decisions backfilled by hand by someone who was in the room — the most valuable day of the month, and it involves no AI. A Slack /predict command.
Week 2
Two extractors, and the first fit
Meta Ads and Shopify. Adjudicator plus the confirmation card. Expect the first fit to be uninformative — at this size wide intervals are the correct answer. Shipping it proves the pipeline, not the numbers.
Week 3
The honest measurement
Hold-one-brand-out against the real ledger. The query surface in Slack, refusal path included. This is the week the thesis gets tested.
Week 4
Allocation, and the calibration leaderboard
Ring-fenced budget agreed with finance; first council. One experiment funded that no single P&L would have paid for.
Deliberately not built in 30 days

WhatsApp and transcript extraction · quick-commerce ingestion · Broadway as a prior source · anything autonomous · cross-category transfer, because the model will almost certainly say it does not transfer and building for it before measuring would be exactly the mistake this system prevents.

A roadmap without exclusions is a wish.

How to tell whether it worked, at day 30

1 · Is the ledger fed without being chased?
2 · Is anyone calibrated? Even one miscalibrated operator identified is worth the month.
3 · Does it beat starting cold on real claims? If not, say so.
4 · Did the Allocator fund something nobody else would have?

North-star metric: cost per validated learning — rupees spent per decision moved from "we're guessing" to "we know." Measurable from the ledger the day it exists. Full plan: docs/roadmap-30-day.md
In closing

Every other asset in the portfolio depreciates. Brand Zero appreciates with every launch.

Inventory, creative, packaging tooling — even the brands themselves. Competitors can buy the same models tomorrow. They cannot buy eleven correlated brands' worth of pre-registered predictions and measured outcomes.

And the honest version of the promise: at eleven brands the ledger and the calibration layer carry the value, pooling compounds later, and the system's most useful habit is telling you when it has nothing to add.
